The part nobody is looking at

The bill is visible. The default that created it is not.

Finance sees inference spend monthly, by provider, by agency, by project. Mature teams already have chargeback, soft limits, hard limits and alerts. All of that reports on a decision that was made weeks ago by someone building an agent.

Most enterprises already have what they need to fix it. Open weights models are approved and available. Some teams have fine tuned checkpoints in production. They run at a fraction of frontier cost and they can do the large majority of agent work.

The obvious answer is to show builders the list of approved models and let them choose. That answer is wrong. More choice at build time slows the builder down and pushes them to the safest looking option, which is almost always the largest one.

The choice does not belong to the builder, and it does not belong at build time. It belongs to a policy, derived from evidence, applied at runtime, where it can be measured and reversed.

One thing to be clear about

The router is a model, not an if condition

It is not a config table with a lookup on top. The router is itself a small model, fine tuned for one job: read the task at runtime and decide where it should go, weighing quality, cost, data access control and sovereignty together. That is why it holds up on work nobody wrote a rule for, and why it improves as your traffic changes rather than going stale the day it ships.

Local is the whole argument

A router that sits outside your environment cannot do this job

Cut the internet and the work should continue, because most of what these agents touch is your own data on your own infrastructure. A router you reach over the public internet breaks that promise on the first call it makes.

01  Nothing leaves

Prompts stay where your agents are

The router runs in your VPC, your cloud account or your rack. Prompts, completions, traces and the routing policy itself never transit a third party. There is no vendor sitting in the path of every call your enterprise makes.

02  It can see more

It knows what you already own

Idle capacity on your own GPUs. The checkpoints your team post trained last quarter. Your own stamped general purpose models, with your compliance rules and the things your brand never says already built in. A hosted router can only choose between the endpoints it can reach.

03  It is auditable

Every choice has a recorded reason

Each routing decision is logged with the task, the candidates considered, the eval evidence, the price per million on each path, and the model that won. Security and finance can read it without asking us for an export.

Four steps, no discovery phase

How the policy gets built

01  Observe

Read the traffic you already have

The router connects to your gateway and traces, then inventories every agent, every task type, and what each one burns per month. Nobody gets interviewed.

02  Simulate

Replay every task against every approved model

Thousands of runs across frontier, general purpose open weights and your own fine tuned checkpoints, scored on your evals and your data, not on a public leaderboard.

03  Publish

Turn the result into a readable policy

Versioned, diffable, and approved through the workflow you already use. Finance sees the cost delta and the eval evidence before a single call moves.

04  Execute

Route at runtime, log the reason

Nobody picks from a dropdown. At build time the router proposes a model and the builder accepts. At runtime the agent calls the same endpoint it always called, the router picks what sits behind it, falls back on failure, and records why.

A task only moves off a frontier model when a smaller one beats it on your evals. When the evidence is not there, the router leaves it alone and says so.

The second bill nobody reads

The same model has three prices, depending on how you bought it

Direct, with the discount you negotiated. Through a cloud marketplace. On a hyperscaler endpoint in a client mandated region. Same weights, three prices. Most builders have never seen the difference, and most finance teams only see it once it has been consolidated into a quarterly number and someone has to get on a call to explain it.

The router prices every path it can reach, benchmarked on one comparable unit of a million tokens, and puts that number at the point of decision.

Enforcement stays where it already is. The request goes through your existing approval workflow with the cost of the choice attached, so the person approving it can see what a model costs before saying yes, instead of hearing about it a quarter later.

Where it does not route

The router's job is knowing which tasks cannot move

Advanced coding

Where the strongest open coding models sit outside your approved list, the frontier model stays. The router will not trade a policy breach for a cost saving.

Heavy agent orchestration

Long multi step runs where a small error at step two compounds by step nine. These stay on frontier reasoning until the evals say otherwise.

Anything without evidence

If no approved alternative beats the incumbent on your own evals, nothing moves. The default answer is always the model you are already using.
